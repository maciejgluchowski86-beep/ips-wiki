\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Calm and facilitating states}
\label{subsec:intro-calm-active}

Many interacting \wiki{spin-system}{spin systems} distinguish sharply
between the roles of their two states.  Throughout the paper we encode
the calm state by \(1\) and the facilitating state by \(0\).  Here
``calm'' describes the influence of a spin on the dynamics around it,
not its own tendency to change.  A site in state \(1\) does not induce
activity at other sites, whereas a site in state \(0\) can cause,
permit, or accelerate an update nearby.

This picture includes several familiar processes whose update rules
look quite different.  In the contact process, written in the
complementary convention in which \(0\) is active, an active site can
give birth at a neighbouring calm site and can itself recover.  In
\wiki{fa-1f-model}{FA-1f}, a facilitating site does not determine the
new state of its neighbour; its presence allows that neighbour to
refresh.  In the
\wiki{babp-model}{biased annihilating branching process} (BABP), every
facilitating neighbour contributes to the rate at which a site flips:
in particle language the same update is either a birth or an
annihilation.  Thus facilitation can act through birth, permission to
refresh, or a direct flip.

The common feature is not a particular choice of rates or update
mechanism.  It is the asymmetry between a state which generates
activity and a state which suppresses it.  Formally, the class studied
below is described by inequalities between the multilinear
coefficients of the flip rates.  These inequalities imply that adding
calm spins decreases the relevant interaction rates.  The coefficient
condition is stronger than coordinatewise monotonicity, but the
calm--facilitating picture is its basic dynamical interpretation.

\subsection{Strong interactions with weak persistence}
\label{subsec:intro-temporal-cancellation}

A facilitating interaction need not be small.  Changing a single
neighbour from \(1\) to \(0\) may turn an update rate from zero to a
quantity of order one, so one spin can completely determine whether
another is allowed to move.  In this usual local sense the interaction
is strong.

Its effect, however, need not persist.  Two consecutive births at the
same site have the same effect as one if the first particle has not
recovered.  Of two consecutive refreshes, the second overwrites the
outcome of the first.  Two consecutive flips cancel.  These mechanisms
are different, but in each case a later interaction can erase the
information carried by an earlier one instead of adding a new layer of
dependence.

There are therefore two distinct notions of interaction strength.  A
single event may depend strongly on the surrounding spins, while a
sequence of such events transmits little persistent information.
Estimates which inspect interactions one at a time, or replace their
contributions by absolute values, measure the first effect and lose
the second.  The aim of the patch construction is to retain precisely
these cancellations in time.

\subsection{Why the standard approaches are insufficient}
\label{subsec:intro-obstructions}

Suppose that independent noise can create facilitating spins
throughout the lattice.  One then expects the system to forget its
initial condition: in a spacetime picture, new sources of activity
appear continually, while the effect of older interactions is
repeatedly overwritten.  This resembles a percolation process with
spontaneously appearing sources and suggests
\wiki{ergodicity}{ergodicity} under very weak noise.

For the contact process this intuition can be supported by
attractiveness and monotone coupling.  The broader
calm--facilitating structure does not imply attractiveness.  A
facilitating spin may increase both directions of an update, as in a
facilitated refresh, or cause alternating births and annihilations, as
in BABP.  Ordered initial configurations can then lose their order,
and the standard upper--lower coupling is unavailable.

Nor do we assume reversibility.  Some of the motivating models are
reversible in their unperturbed form, but the general spin systems
considered here need not be, and adding noise with a different bias
typically destroys the original reversible measure.  Arguments based
on a Dirichlet form, a spectral gap, or entropy dissipation therefore
do not address the intended generality.

There is a natural \wiki{duality}{Feynman--Kac duality} for a general
spin system, but it is signed.  Its weight records the gross strength
of the possible interactions and can be exponentially large when
those interactions are strong.  Taking absolute values in the
duality formula then charges separately for interactions whose effects
later overwrite or cancel.  A useful representation must reorganize
the dual history before its signs and weights are estimated.

\subsection{The patch method}
\label{subsec:intro-method}

The patch method does this by conditioning on a deliberately incomplete
summary of the dual history.  Informally, a dual interaction is called
\wiki{successful-interaction}{successful} when it occurs at a site
which is currently active in the dual and reaches at least one
neighbour.  We retain the time, source, and targets of every such
interaction, but hide the remaining local marks.  This retained
information forms the successful-interaction skeleton.

The skeleton cuts each relevant site-time line at the moments when a
successful interaction enters or leaves the site.  The interval
between two consecutive cuts, together with the way it is touched at
its two ends, is a \wiki{patch}{patch}.  The last patch on a site may
continue forever.  Conditional on the skeleton, the unrevealed
histories inside distinct patches separate into local pieces.  We can
therefore average the sign and Feynman--Kac weight within each patch
and assign a single contribution to the whole interval.

This averaging places two successive interaction boundaries in the
same local calculation.  It can see that a later birth is redundant,
that a later refresh overwrites an earlier one, or that a later flip
reverses it.  Consequently, a patch contribution may be nonnegative
even though the individual dual interactions carry signs and the
unaveraged Feynman--Kac weight is large.  The construction loses no
information: the resulting
\wiki{patch-representation-of-spin-systems}{patch representation} is
an exact rewriting of the original duality formula.

We say that a spin system has \emph{patch positivity} when all
contributions of completed patches are nonnegative.  The main
structural result of the paper characterizes this property by simple
inequalities between the flip-rate coefficients.  These inequalities
hold for a broad class of calm--facilitating systems.  Patch positivity
then supplies the fragment of positive order which ordinary
attractiveness would have provided, and from which the comparison and
convergence results of the paper follow.
