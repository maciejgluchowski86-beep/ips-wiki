\subsection{Facilitation}
\label{subsec:intro-calm-facilitating}
\label{subsec:intro-loss-of-memory}

Some \hyperref[def:spin-system]{spin systems} distinguish calm states from
states which facilitate updates at neighbouring sites. A calm state does not
by itself make another site update, whereas a facilitating state can cause,
permit, or accelerate an update. This distinction appears under different
model-specific names. In the contact process~\cite{Harris1974}, an infected site
infects neighbouring sites and may recover. In
\hyperref[subsubsec:fa-1f-model]{FA-1f}~\cite{BlondelEtAl2013}, a vacancy
permits a neighbouring site to refresh according to an independent Bernoulli
draw, but does not determine the outcome of the refresh. In the
\hyperref[subsubsec:babp-model]{biased annihilating branching process}
(BABP)~\cite{NeuhauserSudbury1993}, a particle causes a neighbouring site to
flip, resulting in branching or annihilation.

A region containing only calm states has no internal source of activity and
tends to remain calm until a facilitating state reaches it. Facilitating states
cause updates, some of which leave further facilitating states behind. This
does not make the dynamics monotone: a refresh or flip can create or remove a
facilitating state, so ordered configurations need not remain ordered. It
nevertheless suggests that some part of the calm--facilitating order may
survive for selected observables.

Suppose now that independent noise creates facilitating states throughout the
lattice. Since these events do not depend on the initial configuration, they
may interrupt the routes through which old information reaches a later local
observation. We ask whether the process, after adding such noise, loses memory
of its initial state and whether the calm--facilitating tendency yields a
useful comparison when attractiveness is absent. Neither monotone coupling nor
reversibility answers these questions in the class considered here. The
contact process is attractive, but FA-1f and BABP need not preserve the order
of two coupled configurations. We also allow nonreversible dynamics, since the
added noise need not preserve a reversible measure of the unperturbed system.
The argument must use the graphical history without ordering trajectories or
identifying the limiting law in advance.

\subsection{Persistence}
\label{subsec:intro-temporal-cancellation}

A facilitating interaction can change a flip rate from zero to a quantity of
order one, and may therefore decide whether a site is allowed to flip. Its
effect can nevertheless disappear before it is transmitted. If no information
leaves a site between two local updates, a second birth is redundant, a later
refresh overwrites the earlier refresh, and two flips cancel. The relevant
quantity is therefore not the instantaneous size of an interaction, but
whether its effect is transmitted before another local update removes it.

The natural \wiki{duality}{Feynman--Kac duality} records the transmission of
influence through a signed set-valued process. Its signs retain cancellations
among successive local events. Taking absolute values before those events are
grouped discards the cancellations and gives only large bounds controlled by
the full interaction rates, even when little influence persists. The dual
history must therefore be decomposed according to the interactions which
transmit information between sites.

\subsection{Patches}
\label{subsec:intro-patches}

Starting from the Feynman--Kac dual of a local observation, we reveal only the
times, sources, and target sets of those dual interactions which transmit
dependence between sites. Their records form the
\wiki{successful-interaction}{successful-interaction skeleton}. The split or
birth kind at a successful interaction, all deaths, and all rings at inactive
sources remain hidden. The one-site intervals between consecutive incoming or
outgoing successful interactions are the \wiki{patch}{patches}. Conditional on
the skeleton, the randomness hidden in distinct patches becomes independent.
The signed Feynman--Kac factors can therefore be averaged patch by patch, and
the resulting contributions give an exact
\wiki{patch-representation-of-spin-systems}{representation} of the original
spin system. This averaging retains sign cancellations between successive
interactions.

We say that a spin system has \emph{patch positivity} when every bulk patch
contribution is nonnegative. This condition is characterized by explicit
inequalities between the multilinear coefficients of the flip rates. It does
not imply stochastic order, but it yields comparisons for centered moments,
and the semigroup preserves the resulting moment order. The same
representation compares a system with the system obtained by removing an
independent pure-death component. When such deaths occur at a uniformly
positive rate, local observables converge exponentially to a common invariant
limit, which can be described by a patch formula.
