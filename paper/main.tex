\subsection{Calm regions and facilitating activity}
\label{subsec:intro-calm-facilitating}
\label{subsec:intro-loss-of-memory}

Some \hyperref[def:spin-system]{spin systems} distinguish between calm states and
states which facilitate activity in their neighbors. A calm
spin does not by itself make another site update. Facilitating activity can
cause, permit, or accelerate such an update.

This distinction appears under different update rules. In the contact
process, an infected infects neighboring sites and may recover. In
\hyperref[subsubsec:fa-1f-model]{FA-1f}, a vacancy permits a neighbor to
refresh its state according to an independent Bernoulli draw, but does not
determine the outcome of the refresh. In the
\hyperref[subsubsec:babp-model]{biased annihilating branching process} (BABP), a
particle causes a neighboring site to flip, resulting in branching or
annihilation. In each case, the state at one site determines which changes are
possible at another.

A region containing only calm states has no internal source of activity and
tends to remain calm until activity reaches it. Facilitating states instead
cause updates, some of which leave further facilitating states behind. This
does not make the dynamics monotone: a refresh or flip can create or remove activity, so ordered configurations need not
remain ordered. It nevertheless suggests that some part of the
calm-facilitating order may survive for selected observables.

Suppose now that independent noise creates facilitating activity throughout
the lattice. Since these events do not depend on the initial configuration,
they may interrupt the routes through which old information reaches a later
local observation. We ask whether the process, after adding some noise to it, loses memory of its initial
state, and whether the calm-facilitating tendency yields a useful comparison
when attractiveness is absent.

Neither monotone coupling nor reversibility answers these questions in the
class considered here. The contact process is attractive, but FA-1f and BABP
need not preserve the order of two coupled configurations. We also allow
nonreversible dynamics, since noise need not preserve a
reversible measure of the unperturbed system. The argument must use the
graphical history without ordering trajectories or identifying the limiting
law in advance.

\subsection{Interaction strength and persistence}
\label{subsec:intro-temporal-cancellation}

A facilitating interaction can change a flip rate from zero to a quantity of
order one, and may therefore decide whether a site is allowed to flip. Its
effect can nevertheless disappear before it is transmitted. If no information
leaves a site between two local updates, a second birth is redundant, a
later refresh overwrites the earlier refresh, and two flips cancel.

The natural \wiki{duality}{Feynman-Kac duality} records the transmission of
influence through a signed set-valued process. Its signs retain cancellations
among successive local events. Taking absolute values before those events are
grouped discards the cancellations and gives only large bounds controlled by the full
interaction rates, even when little influence persists. The dual history must
therefore be decomposed according to the interactions which transmit
information between sites.

\subsection{Patches and recovered order}
\label{subsec:intro-patches}

Starting from a local observation and tracing its graphical history backwards,
we reveal the times and endpoints of the interactions which carry dependence
from one site to another. These interactions form the
\wiki{successful-interaction}{successful-interaction skeleton}. The remaining
local Poisson marks are left hidden. Between successive touches of the
skeleton, the history splits into one-site time intervals which we call
\wiki{patch}{patches}.

Conditional on the skeleton, the randomness hidden in distinct patches
becomes independent. The signed Feynman-Kac factors can be averaged patch by patch, and
the resulting contributions give an exact
\wiki{patch-representation-of-spin-systems}{representation} of the original
spin system. This averaging retains cancellations of sign between successive interactions.

We say that a spin system has \emph{patch positivity} when every bulk patch
contribution is nonnegative. This condition is characterized by explicit
inequalities between the multilinear coefficients of the flip rates. It does
not imply stochastic order, but it yields comparison for the centered moments. The semigroup preserves the resulting
moment order. The same representation also compares a system with the system
obtained by removing an independent pure-death component. When such deaths
occur at a uniformly positive rate, local observables converge exponentially
to a common invariant limit, which can be described by a patch formula.
