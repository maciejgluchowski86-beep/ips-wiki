\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Calm and facilitating spin systems}
\label{subsec:intro-calm-facilitating}

Many interacting \wiki{spin-system}{spin systems} distinguish sharply
between the roles of their two states.  Throughout the paper we encode
the calm state by \(1\) and the facilitating state by \(0\).  Here
``calm'' describes the influence of a spin on the dynamics around it,
not its own tendency to change.  A calm site does not generate activity
at other sites, whereas a facilitating site can cause, permit, or
accelerate an update nearby.

This picture includes familiar processes with quite different update
rules.  In the contact process, written in the complementary convention
in which \(0\) is active, an active site can give birth at a
neighbouring calm site and can itself recover.  In
\wiki{fa-1f-model}{FA-1f}, a facilitating site does not determine the
new state of its neighbour; its presence permits that neighbour to
refresh.  In the
\wiki{babp-model}{biased annihilating branching process} (BABP), every
facilitating neighbour contributes to the rate at which a site flips,
so the same interaction is a birth or an annihilation according to the
current state of the target.  Facilitation can thus act through birth,
permission to refresh, or a direct flip.

The common feature is an asymmetry between a state which generates
activity and one which suppresses it, rather than a particular update
mechanism or choice of rates.  The formal condition used below is
expressed through the multilinear coefficients of the flip rates.  Its
negative interaction coefficients imply that adding calm spins
decreases the relevant update rates.  This is stronger than
coordinatewise decrease, but the calm--facilitating picture is its
basic dynamical interpretation.

The motivating question is what becomes of these systems when weak
independent noise creates facilitating spins throughout the lattice.
Does the continuing supply of fresh activity make the process forget
its initial state?  More generally, when the dynamics is not
attractive, does any useful comparison structure survive?  These are
the two questions addressed in this paper.

\subsection{Noise and loss of memory}
\label{subsec:intro-loss-of-memory}

If every site can become facilitating at a fixed positive rate, new
sources of activity appear continually and independently of the past.
One expects these sources to interrupt the paths along which the
initial configuration can influence the future.  The spacetime picture
resembles percolation with spontaneously appearing sources: even
arbitrarily weak noise should eventually separate a local observation
from sufficiently old information.  The desired conclusion is a
quantitative form of \wiki{ergodicity}{loss of memory}: local
observables should converge to the same limiting values, uniformly over
an appropriate class of initial laws.

For the contact process, attractiveness makes this intuition accessible
through monotone coupling.  The wider calm--facilitating class need not
be attractive.  A facilitating spin may enable both directions of a
refresh, as in FA-1f, or cause alternating births and annihilations, as
in BABP.  Ordered initial configurations can then lose their order, so
the usual construction of upper and lower trajectories which trap all
other evolutions is unavailable.

We also do not assume reversibility.  Some motivating examples are
reversible before they are perturbed, but a general facilitating spin
system need not be, and noise with a different bias need not preserve
the original reversible measure.  Methods built around a known
reversible law and its functional inequalities therefore do not apply
uniformly to the class considered here.

The calm--facilitating picture nevertheless suggests more structure
than these general obstructions reveal.  Fresh activity is created
without reference to the past, and the interactions it triggers often
replace earlier effects instead of accumulating them.  The problem is
to retain this temporal cancellation without assuming a monotone
coupling or a reversible measure.

\subsection{Strong interactions with weak persistence}
\label{subsec:intro-temporal-cancellation}

A facilitating interaction need not be small.  Changing one neighbour
from \(1\) to \(0\) may turn an update rate from zero to a quantity of
order one, so a single spin can completely determine whether another
site is allowed to move.  In the usual sense of local sensitivity, this
is a strong interaction.

Its effect need not be equally persistent.  If no information is
transmitted in between, a second birth at an already active site is
redundant, a second refresh overwrites the outcome of the first, and a
second flip reverses it.  In an interacting system the intermediate
state may of course affect other sites, so these cancellations cannot
simply be imposed event by event.  They become visible only after one
records which interactions actually carry information elsewhere and
then averages over the local history between them.

Thus instantaneous interaction strength and persistence of influence
are different quantities.  A sequence of events may depend strongly on
the surrounding spins at every step while transmitting little
information over a long time.  This distinction is hidden by estimates
which treat each interaction separately.

The natural \wiki{duality}{Feynman--Kac duality} illustrates the
problem.  For a general spin system the dual is signed, and its
Feynman--Kac weight measures the gross strength of the interactions.
When the rates depend strongly on neighbouring spins, this weight can
be large.  Taking absolute values then charges separately for events
whose effects later overwrite or reverse one another.  Small-weight
duality estimates therefore see the instantaneous strength but not the
weak persistence.  To recover the latter, the dual history has to be
regrouped before its signs and weights are estimated.

\subsection{Patches and recovered positivity}
\label{subsec:intro-patches}

The patch construction is such a regrouping.  Imagine tracing backwards
the possible routes by which a local observation can depend on the
initial configuration.  Some graphical interactions extend the
currently explored history from one site to another.  We record the
times and endpoints of these information-carrying interactions, but
temporarily hide the remaining local randomness.  The recorded data
form what will later be called the
\wiki{successful-interaction}{successful-interaction skeleton}.

The skeleton divides the explored history into one-site time intervals
bounded by consecutive interactions.  These intervals, together with
the ways in which they are touched at their ends, are the
\wiki{patch}{patches}.  Once the skeleton is fixed, the hidden histories
inside different patches separate.  Their signs and Feynman--Kac
weights can therefore be averaged one patch at a time.  The product of
the resulting patch contributions, averaged over the skeleton, gives
an exact representation of the original spin system.

The important point is that one patch contains the local history
between two interaction boundaries.  Its contribution can retain the
cancellation between an update and the next update that carries
information, whereas a termwise estimate of the dual cannot.  A patch
contribution may consequently be nonnegative even when the individual
dual events have signs and the ungrouped Feynman--Kac weight is large.
No approximation is made: the
\wiki{patch-representation-of-spin-systems}{patch representation}
reorganizes the original duality formula without changing it.

We call the resulting property \emph{patch positivity}: all
contributions of closed patches are nonnegative.  The main structural
result characterizes patch positivity by simple inequalities between
the flip-rate coefficients, and these inequalities express the
calm--facilitating structure described above.  Patch positivity is not
attractiveness and does not recover stochastic order.  It gives
positivity for the grouped dual weights and, through them, monotonicity
and comparison for a restricted family of moments.  This sliver of
order is enough to obtain the convergence results under noise and the
full-patch description of the limiting law developed later in the
paper.
